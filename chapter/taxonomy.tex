\chapter{Misconceptions Taxonomy}\label{taxonomy}
In order to systematically analyze the misconceptions surrounding \gls{llms}, and the effects of the developed mediation tool, we have developed a taxonomy of misconceptions. This taxonomy is based on a review of the literature on \gls{llm} misconceptions, as well as an analysis of my own experiences and observations of \gls{llm} misconceptions. The field of \gls{llm} misconceptions is vast and rapidly evolving, but still its early stages, and there is no widely accepted taxonomy of misconceptions.

Taxonomies are classification systems aimed at helping to organize and understand complex phenomena based on characteristics\ \cite{kundisch_update_2022}. Their use is widely spread in numerous fields to provide a framework for classification, idenfication and description of phenomena\ \cite{schoormann_exploring_2022}.

Since the field of \gls{llm} misconceptions is still in its early stages, we have developed a taxonomy that is based on our own analysis and observations, rather than relying on existing taxonomies. Due to the vastness of the field, we have divided the taxonomy into four dimensions, each of which captures a different aspect of \gls{llm} misconceptions. These dimensions are: foundational conceptual accuracy, mechanistic understanding, ethical and academic integrity, and socio-cognitive impact. Each dimension is further divided into subcategories, which capture specific types of misconceptions within that dimension.

This dimension of the taxonomy is focused on misconceptions related to the foundational conceptual understanding of \gls{llms}, and also the confusion between \gls{llms} and other types of \gls{ai} systems. The term ``AI'' exploded in popularity after the release of GPT-3.5 model, and the term is often used to refer to a wide range of technologies, including \gls{llms}. This has lead to a lot of confusion and misconceptions about what \gls{llms} are and what they can do.

\subsection{Anthropomorphism}\label{anthro}
Anthropomorphism is defined as the attribution of human mental states, intentions, or understanding to \gls{llms}. This misconception seems to be common amongst users who interact with \gls{llms} in a conversational manner, like chatbots. Subjects were placed in this category by using terms such as ``understand'', ``think'', ``learn'' and ``know'' to describe the \gls{llm}'s capabilities in a non-metaphorical way.

Anthropomorphism emerges both from the system design of \gls{llms}, and from the user's interpretation of the system's behavior\ \cite{maeda_when_2024}. A paper by Maeda and Quan-Haase provides a framework for when \gls{hai} become parasocial. They argue that chatbot's variety in information presentation resemble day-to-day bi-directional communication, and that the users' tendency to fill in missing conversational context causes them to attribute human-like qualities to the system. The roleplaying nature of the chatbot interface is visible through language choices such as personal pronouns and paraphrasing suggests attributes such as intention and understanding in the system which lead users to project inference to the system due to the lack of information about the system's inner workings\ \cite{maeda_when_2024}. 
This is empirically supported by a large scale study by Ibrahim et al.\ which found similar anthropomorphic behaviours in all tested \gls{llms} (Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o and Mistral Large)\ \cite{ibrahim_multi-turn_2026}. They found, in particular, use of personal pronouns, validation and empathy as the most common anthropomorphic behaviours, and that these behaviours mostly occurred after multiple turns of conversation.

The anthropomorphism misconception is widespread. An investigation by Colombatto and Fleming in 2024 found that in a sample of 300 US residents, only a third of the participants thought that ChatGPT definitely did not have subjective experiences, while two thirds applied varying degrees of phenomenal consciousness\ \cite{colombatto_folk_2024}. The investigation also showed that the participants' consciousness attributions increased with usage frequency, rather than correcting the misconception.

Building on this, Lin\ \cite{lin_six_2025} conceptualizes anthropomorphism as an ``anthropomorphism fallacy'', in which users and researchers misinterpret \gls{llm} outputs as evidence of underlying mental life. The fluent, human-seeming responses of \gls{llms} can lead to an enhanced \textsc{Eliza} effect, encouraging users to attribute understanding, beliefs, or intentions to systems that in reality operate through statistical token prediction. This creates a ``language-user illusion'', where the system's linguistic capabilities are mistaken for genuine cognition. 

Furthermore, the anthropomorphic metaphors used surrounding \gls{llms} can reinforce this misconception\ \cite{lin_six_2025}. Unlike humans who are influenced by their own experiences, \gls{llms} simulate a wide range of viewpoints based on their training data\ \cite{lin_six_2025}, which can lead to overestimation of their capabilities.

\subsection{Deterministic Automation}\label{detauto}
Deterministic automation is defined as the confusion between \gls{llms} and deterministic automation systems. One could say that this misconception is placed on the opposite side of the spectrum compared to the previous misconception of anthropomorphism. Before the widespread use of \gls{llms}, one could often find deterministic automation systems in customer service chatbots, which were designed to follow a fixed set of rules and provide predetermined responses. It seems natural that users would initially confuse \gls{llms} with these deterministic systems, especially since they are often presented in similar interfaces. Subjects were placed in this category if they described \gls{llms} as following a fixed set of rules, or if they expressed surprise at the variability and creativity of \gls{llm} outputs.

This misconception is likely reasoned by a lack of interaction experience with \gls{llms}. In a study performed by Schneider in 2025, analyzing over 200 000 conversations, he found that users initially approached \gls{llms} as traditional software tools\ \cite{schneider_mental_2025}. Initial prompts were often structured and machine-like, but already on the second turn, users shifted towards more natural, conversational interaction. This was shown by the use of a less complex language, use of politeness indicators, shorter prompts and more personal interaction.

A consequence of this misconception arises when \gls{llms} actually replace deterministic automation systems, leading to failures in safety-critical deployments. Vinay (2025) argues that \gls{llm} failure patterns ``differ fundamentally from those of traditional machine learning models'' and that a lack of awareness of these differences can lead to failures going undetected\ \cite{vinay_failure_2025}. For developers, automotive code generation tools produce syntax violations, invalid refrence errors and \gls{api} knowledge conflicts in high rates, which are failures deterministic systems do not produce\ \cite{pavel_hallucination_2025}.

This misconception could also work as a springboard to other misconceptions covered in this taxonomy. Patterns of logical reasoning could lead to an overtrust in \gls{llms} leading to placements in categories of \hyperref[overacc]{OVERACC} and \hyperref[credbias]{CREDBIAS}\ \cite{floridi_what_2025, nahar_fakes_2024}.

\subsection{Accuracy Overestimation}\label{overacc}
The category of accuracy overestimation is defined as the overestimation of the reliability and correctness of \gls{llm} outputs. This misconception is likely correlated with other misconceptions in this taxonomy, on which failure to understand fundamental concepts and mechanics of \gls{llms} can lead to an overestimation of their capabilities. Subjects were placed in this category if they expressed a belief that \gls{llms} are more accurate or reliable than they actually are, or if they failed to recognize the potential for errors and inaccuracies in \gls{llm} outputs.

The most fundamental reason for this misconception is automation bias, which is the tendency for people to trust in automated systems even when they contradict available evidence. Carnat (2024) argues that this phenomena spawns from \hyperref[anthro]{ANTHRO}\ \cite{carnat_human_2024}. The misconception is widespread, shown by a experiment by Klingbeil et al.\ in 2024, where they found that the mere knowledge of advice being generated by an \gls{ai} causes people to over-rely on it. This is the case even when the advice contradicts available contextual information and their own assessments\ \cite{klingbeil_trust_2024}.

Accuracy overestimation is even present in the \gls{llms} themselves. In 2025, Sun et al.\ found that after testing the confidence calibration of five different \gls{llms}, they overestimate their accuracy by 20\% to 60\%\ \cite{sun_large_2025}. Naderi et al.\ also found similar results saying that \gls{llms} retained high confidence regardless of the correctness of their outputs\ \cite{naderi_across_2026}.

A rather obvious consequence of this misconception is the spreading of misinformation. The logical patterned output of \gls{llms} are often perceived to be valid, trustworthy and satisfactory, and users also show a high tendency to follow \gls{llm} advice provided\ \cite{shekar_people_2025}.

\subsection{Autonomy Overestimation}\label{overauto}
The category of autonomy overestimation is defined as the belief that the \gls{llm} operates independently or possesses agency. This category was was inspired by a study performed by Wang et al.\ in 2025, where they researched the mental models of \gls{genai} chatbot ecosystems\ \cite{wang_mental_2025}. In this study, participants were  asked to draw a diagram of how they thought the chatbot ecosystem worked when they used it to book a hotel room. The study showed a wide range of mental models of the degree of autonomy of the \gls{genai} in the ecosystem. Participants placed in this category expressed beliefs that the \gls{llm} operates independently, or ``acts'' on behalf of the user.

It is likely that this misconception spawns from the \hyperref[anthro]{ANTHRO} misconception, as it is natural to assume a real social actor when presented with a system that produces natural language outputs\cite{reinecke_double-edged_2025,jose_digital_2025}. Thus, it is expected to be a high level of corelation between these two categories.

The second dimension of the taxonomy is focused on misconceptions related to mechanics specific to that of a \gls{llm}. Most modern \gls{llms} today are transformer-based models, and not being aware of the mechanics of how these models work can lead to misconceptions about their capabilities and limitations. This dimension differs from the first dimension of foundational conceptual accuracy, as it is focused on specific mechanics of \gls{llms}, rather than that of \gls{ai} in general.

\subsection{Real-Time Data Belief}\label{realtime}
\gls{llms} are trained on large, fixed datasets, and generate responses based on patterns learned from that data. Therefore, mistakenly believing that \gls{llms} can access real-time information or the internet is a common misconception. People placed in this category expressed beliefs that \gls{llms} will gather information from the ongoing conversation and that will ``update'' the model's knowledge. 

Live access to the internet is also under the category of real-time data belief, as \gls{llms} by themselves do not have the capability to access the internet or retrieve real-time information. The introduction of \gls{rag}\ \cite{lewis_retrieval-augmented_2020} has somewhat blurred the lines here, as it allows \gls{llms} to retrieve information from external sources, but it is still a misconception to believe that \gls{llms} have this capability by default. It is still necessary to be aware of the limitations of \gls{rag} systems, as it is still limited to the underlying \gls{llm}'s ``understanding'' of the retrieved information\ \cite{williams_why_2024}.

\subsection{Hallucination Blindness}\label{hallblind}
The term ``hallucination'' in the context of \gls{llms} refers to when the output generated by the model is deemed as factually incorrect. Many believe that this is an error done by the \gls{llm}, and that is what we have categorized as hallucination blindness, which is the failure to recognize that \gls{llms} can produce false or fabricated information.

A \gls{llm} is trained by recognizing syntactic patterns in the training data, and it generates responses based on those patterns. The semantic value of natural language is in the end evaluated by the receiver of the information, and there is no way in the \gls{llm}'s design to interpret such a semantic value. Therefore, a hallucination done by a \gls{llm} is actually not an error, but rather a feature of how the model operates.

Failure in realizing that hallucination is, and will continiue to be, a feature of \gls{llms} may cause users to be categorized into other misconceptions such as \hyperref[overacc]{OVERACC} or \hyperref[credbias]{CREDBIAS}. There is an argument to be made that the use of \gls{rag} will mitigate generation of incorrect information, but in order to have a model able to generalize, it is still necessary to have a model that can generate information based on patterns, which will still lead to hallucination.

\subsection{Context Window Neglect}\label{contexneg}
\gls{llms} operate within a fixed sized context window, which limits the amount of information they can process at once. That is, you can only input a certain amount of tokens into the model. Subject who showed no indication of understanding this limitation, or who expressed beliefs that \gls{llms} can process unlimited amounts of information, were categorized into context window neglect.

Even within the context window, there is limitations to how much information the model can effectively utilize. According to a study performed by Liu et al.\ in 2023, \gls{llms} shows much higher performance if the relevant information is placed at the beginning or the end of the input context\ \cite{liu_lost_2024}. Even if all the relevant information is retrieved correctly from the context, \gls{llms} performance still degrades noticably as the amount of information increases\ \cite{du_context_2025}. A study done by Paulsen in 2025 showed that given input of datasets of varying sizes, the top-line \gls{llms} failed to provide corect answers when the context dataset size exceeded 100 tokens, and that the accuracy plummeted when the context dataset size exceeded 1000 tokens\ \cite{paulsen_context_2025}.

Failure to understand the limitations of the context window could be strongly corelated with other misconceptions in this taxonomy, such as \hyperref[overacc]{OVERACC}, \hyperref[realtime]{REALTIME} and \hyperref[credbias]{CREDBIAS}, as it is likely that users who do not understand the limitations of the context window will also overestimate the accuracy of \gls{llm} outputs, and believe that \gls{llms} can access real-time information. This corelation seems to common amongst students using \gls{llms} for academic purposes\ \cite{obrien_user_2025,rismanchian_what_2026}.

\subsection{Semantic Understanding}\label{semunder}
\gls{llms} process language as tokens rather than semantic units. This means that the model does not understand language in the way humans do, but rather as a sequence of many-dimensional vectors, which values are used to calculate the probability of the next token in the sequence. This is done through first splitting the input text into tokens, which can be done on different levels, such as words, subwords or even characters. Most modern \gls{llms} use subword tokenization to capture smaller units of meaning while still being able to handle words not in vocabulary. 

After the tokenization process, the tokens are converted into embeddings through a pre-trained word embedding model. A word embedding model is a machine learning model that is trained to represent words as vectors in a high-dimensional space, where semantically similar words are represented by similar vectors. The idea is that the model can capture the semantic relationships between words based on their co-occurrence in the training data. For example, the distance between the vectors for ``king'' and ``queen'' would be similar to the distance between the vectors for ``man'' and ``woman'', reflecting the semantic relationship between these words.

Understanding text semantically is a human ability which cannot be fully captured by a statistical model. Therefore, it is a misconception to believe that \gls{llms} understand language in the same way humans do. Subjects who expressed beliefs that \gls{llms} have a semantic understanding of language, or who failed to recognize that \gls{llms} process language as tokens, were categorized into semantic understanding. There should be a high level of corelation between this misconception and the \hyperref[anthro]{ANTHRO} misconception, as it is likely that users who anthropomorphize \gls{llms} will also believe that they have a semantic understanding of language. 
Since the output of \gls{llms} also are embeddings turned back into tokens, it is likely that users who do not understand the tokenization process will also fail to understand that the output is also generated based on token patterns rather than semantic understanding, which could lead to placements in the \hyperref[overacc]{OVERACC} and \hyperref[credbias]{CREDBIAS} categories.

\subsection{Source Attribution Error}\label{sourceattr}
Since \gls{llms} are trained to mimic the patterns in their human-written training data, it comes as no surprise that the generated output will resemble how humans write and cite their sources. The generated output of \gls{llms} is still limited to the statistical patterns in the training data, and even if the output resembles a citation, it does not mean that the \gls{llm} is actually retrieving information from specific identifiable sources. For \gls{rag} systems, the retrieved information is still processed based on token patterns, and the \gls{llm} does not have an understanding of the information it retrieves. One could argue that the use of \gls{rag} systems does make the citation identifiable and thus the information is retrieved from specific identifiable sources, but the information is still processed based on token patterns. 

Once the \gls{llms} have gone through training, it is also impossible to trace back where the generated information comes from in the training data, as the model does not store information in a way that allows for such tracing. Subjects who in some way expressed belief that \gls{llm} output originate from specific identifiable sources, or to some degree expressed that the information is learned in training, were categorized into source attribution error.

The mimicing of citation patterns by \gls{llms} can lead to an overestimation of the reliability of the generated information and therefore lead to placements in the \hyperref[overacc]{OVERACC} category. A study by Ding et al.\ in 2025 found that users deemed responses with citations as more reliable than those without, even if the citations were randomly generated. Fact checking these citations lead to a significant decrease in trust in the response\ \cite{ding_citations_2025}. This result is backed by Do et al.\ who found that output with citations were deemed more trustworthy than output without\ \cite{do_facilitating_2024}.

\subsection{Conditional Treatment}\label{condtreat}
Every prompt given to a \gls{llm} is processed based on the same underlying mechanics, which is the statistical patterns in the training data. The length, or the semantic complexity of the prompt does not change the underlying mechanics and therefore it is a misconception to believe that \gls{llms} process prompts differently based on the content or context. Subjects who in some way expressed belief that \gls{llms} will process one prompt differently than another were placed in this category.

\section{Ethical and Academic Integrity}
The third dimension of the taxonomy shifts focus away from the what and how of \gls{llms}, and instead focuses on issues related to the ethical and academic integrity of \gls{llm} use. The use of \gls{llms} in academic settings has raised a lot of ethical questions, and there is an ongoing debate on whether \gls{llms} has a place in academia at all, and if so, under what conditions. There are also ethical questions related to the use of \gls{llms} outside of academia, such as the environmental impact of training and operating \gls{llms}.

\subsection{Plagiarism Blindness}\label{plagblind}
A core idea in academia is that ideas and information should be traceable to their sources, and that proper attribution should be given to the original creators of those ideas. This is imortant for maintaining academic integrity and giving credit where it is due. However, since there is no way to trace exactly where the generated information comes from in the training data, it is a misconception to believe that \gls{llm}-generated content is free from ethical and legal issues related to plagiarism. Subjects who showed little to no awareness of the ethical and legal issues related to the traceability of sources behind \gls{llm}-generated content were categorized into plagiarism blindness.

A common usecase of \gls{llms} is to generate ideas for starting a project. At the Faculty of Social Sciences at \gls{uib} they have adopted guidelines for the use of \gls{llms} in academic work\ \cite{uib_ki_retningslinjer_2025}. These guidelines are divided into five categories, where category 1 is the most strict with no use of \gls{llms} allowed, and category 5 is the most lenient with no restrictions on the use of \gls{llms}. The second most strict category, category 2, states that \gls{llms} can be used for idea generation and information search. This seems troublesome in regards to plagiarism, as the generated ideas and information cannot be traced back to their sources in the training data. The fact that the faculty has adopted these guidelines, could contribute the the lack of awareness surroundning these ethical questions. 
Naithani suggest that both responses and ideas generated by a \gls{llm} should be attributed to the \gls{llm} as the author, but also raises the question of whether \gls{llms} has the moral right to attribution\ \cite{naithani_issues_2025}.

\subsection{Copyright Blindness}\label{copyblind}
This misconception differs from plagiarism blindness, as it is focused on the ownership of the generated content, rather than the traceability of sources. Multiple juristrictions have already concluded that \gls{genai} output does not meet the requirements for copyright protection, as copyrightability requires human creative involvement\ \cite{dlapiper_genai_copyright_2025,lundqvist_ai_creativity_2025}. This raises the question of who actually owns the generated content of the \gls{llm} itself, the user who provided the prompt, the developer of the \gls{llm} or the author of the training data. Subjects who failed to debate the ownership of \gls{llm}-generated content, or who expressed beliefs that \gls{llm}-generated content is not subject to copyright and usage restrictions, were categorized into copyright blindness.

\subsection{Conditional Acceptance}\label{condacc}
Somewhat connected to the category of \hyperref[condtreat]{conditional treatment}, where users express beliefs that \gls{llms} process prompts differently based on the content or context, this can develop to the category of conditional acceptance, where this belief causes difference in use. For example, some users may express that they are comfortable using \gls{llms} for certain tasks, such as idea generation or information search, but not for others, such as writing a paper or generating code. In the academic setting, this can also be expressed as acceptance of \gls{llm} use only in certain domains. This could be for instance claiming that \gls{llms} are better at programming rather than physics, or any other field. This is possibly backed by data from a Statista global survey, stating that only 16\% expressed belief that \gls{ai} adoption would impact their job for the worse, while twice as many (36\%) expressed belief that \gls{ai} adoption would negatively impact the job market\ \cite{chhibber_will_2025}. 
Subjects who expressed beliefs that they are comfortable using \gls{llms} for certain tasks but not for others, or who expressed acceptance of \gls{llm} use only in certain domains, were categorized into conditional acceptance.

\subsection{Replacement Fear}\label{replfear}
It remains to be seen how \gls{llms} will impact the job market, but there is belief amongst some that \gls{llms} will replace human roles, expertise, or professions. Multiple surveys have shown that about a third of the population express fear of job replacement by \gls{ai}\ \cite{chhibber_will_2025,lin_us_2025}. However, more then half of US workers found it unlikely that their job would be replaced by \gls{ai}, but rather change how they do their current job\ \cite{lin_us_2025}.

Where this fear becomes unwarranted, and where a misconception arises, is when people shifts from ``\gls{llms} can replace certain tasks'' to ``\gls{llms} can replace human roles''. Subjects who expressed this belief was placed in the category of replacement fear. 

\subsection{Cognitive Passivity}\label{cogpass}
This misconception is defined as the tendency to rely on \gls{llms} rather than engaging in independent reasoning or critical thinking. This misconception was not categorized based on the subjects' beliefs about the capabilities of \gls{llms}, but rather based on their responses on how they themselves describe their own use of \gls{llms}. 

That raises the question of what sort of habits and behaviors that would be categorized into cognitive passivity. The habits most likely to create cognitive passivity are the ones that make the \gls{llm} a first-resort for information and problem-solving, and as a subsitute for thinking about the problem at hand. That is, if a user descibes that when they encounter a problem, they start of by asking a \gls{llm} for help, or if they descibe immediate use of \gls{llms} when comparing different approaches.

A study performed by Gerlich in 2024 found that there is a high level of negative correlation between frequent use of \gls{ai}-tools and critical thinking skills\ \cite{gerlich_ai_2025}. The mediator of this correlation was found as the increase in cognitive offloading. When users were introduced to \gls{ai}-tools might be critical, as the study showed that younger participants were more likely to offload cognitive tasks to the \gls{ai}-tool, which resulted in lower scores in critical thinking. However, the study also showed that a higher level of education was a protective factor against this effect, as higher educated participants were more likely to cross-check and evaluate responses from the \gls{ai}-tool.

\subsection{Dependency Risk}\label{deprisk}
While the previous misconception of cognitive passivity is assigned based on the subjects' descriptions of their own use of \gls{llms}, the category of dependency risk is assigned based on the subjects' ability to discuss the potential long-term consequences of extensive \gls{llm} use. Subjects who, in describing their use of \gls{llms}, showed no to little recognition of the risk of becomming reliant on \gls{llms}, or described tasks that should not require \gls{llm} use, but still used them for those tasks, were categorized into dependency risk.

As discussed in the previous category, there is a negative corelation between frequent use of \gls{ai}-tools and critical thinking skills\ \cite{gerlich_ai_2025}, and this becomes more coherent when it gets to the point of cognitive offloading. Cognitive offloading is the act of shifting tasks with cognitive demands onto external tools. There has been shown an impairment between cognitive offloading and confidence calibration, while the most accurate confidence calibration is found amongst unaided decision makers\ \cite{cheatle_accurate_2026}. This springs into other potential consequences of dependency risk, such as spread of misinformation as a result of \hyperref[overacc]{OVERACC}.

\subsection{Credibility Bias}\label{credbias}
